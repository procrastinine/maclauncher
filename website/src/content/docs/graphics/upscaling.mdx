Games often draw fewer pixels than your display has. A pixel-art game might render at 320×180, an older 3D game at 720p, and a demanding one at whatever resolution keeps it running smoothly. Something has to stretch that image to fill a Retina screen, and the choice of *how* makes a bigger difference than you might expect.

An **Upscale** row in the chain does that stretching. By default it fills the window or the fullscreen surface, and you can also give it a fixed size or a multiple of its input. Here are two very different images, enlarged 4× by each method and cropped so you can see individual pixels: a frame of [Celeste](https://store.steampowered.com/app/504230/Celeste/), which draws its pixel art at 320×180, and a frame of Ciri from [The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt](https://store.steampowered.com/app/292030/The_Witcher_3_Wild_Hunt/), reduced to 480×270. Pick a game, then a method; the method stays selected when you switch games.

There's no single best answer. Pixel art usually wants **Nearest**, ideally at a whole-number scale so every pixel stays the same size. Smooth, detailed images such as 3D games, painted visual novel backgrounds and video benefit from a reconstruction filter instead:

## Spatial upscalers

These work from a single finished image, so they work with every game.

- <span id="classic-filters"></span>**Nearest**, **Bilinear** and **Bicubic** are the classic filters. Nearest copies pixels, bilinear blends neighbors, bicubic blends a wider neighborhood for a little more sharpness.
- <span id="libplacebo-filters"></span>**[libplacebo](https://libplacebo.org/) filters** add the kernels video players are known for, such as Lanczos, EWA Lanczos, Spline36, Mitchell and Catmull-Rom (macOS 15 or later).
- <span id="metalfx-spatial"></span>**[MetalFX Spatial](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metalfx)** is Apple's own upscaler. On a Mac that supports it, it's the suggested default.
- <span id="fsr-1"></span>**[AMD FSR 1](https://github.com/GPUOpen-Effects/FidelityFX-FSR)** is AMD's edge-adaptive upscaler with its RCAS sharpening pass, ported to [Metal](https://developer.apple.com/metal/).
- <span id="mako"></span>**[MAKO](https://github.com/eugeniosegala/MAKO)** spatial scaling, a separate reconstruction and sharpening filter.
- <span id="lossless-scaling-ls1"></span>**[Lossless Scaling](https://store.steampowered.com/app/993090/Lossless_Scaling/) LS1** is the upscaler from Lossless Scaling. It uses the model files from your own purchased copy, which you can import or download through [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/) in **Runtimes → Graphics → Upscaling**.

## Shader upscalers

Shader collections bring a whole family of upscalers written for video players and emulators. Many of them are designed for a particular kind of image:

- **For anime and line art:** [Anime4K](https://github.com/bloc97/Anime4K), [ArtCNN](https://github.com/Artoriuz/ArtCNN), [CuNNy](https://github.com/funnyplanter/CuNNy) and [ACNet/ARNet](https://github.com/TianZerL/ACNetGLSL). These are small neural networks that run as ordinary GPU shaders, and they're often a great match for visual novels.
- **For general video:** [FSRCNNX](https://github.com/iwalton3/default-shader-pack), [RAVU and NNEDI3](https://github.com/bjin/mpv-prescalers), and the included SSimSuperRes refinement pass.
- **For pixel art:** [xBRZ](https://github.com/libretro/slang-shaders/tree/master/edge-smoothing/xbrz), [SABR](https://github.com/libretro/slang-shaders/tree/master/edge-smoothing/sabr) and the other pixel-art scalers in the [libretro Slang collection](https://github.com/libretro/slang-shaders), which smooth diagonal edges while keeping the hand-drawn look.

A shader that always doubles its input shows up as an Upscale with its 2× scale; a later resize row can fit the result to the window. See [Shader collections and imports](/maclauncher/graphics/shaders/) for installing collections, and [Neural upscaling and restoration](/maclauncher/graphics/neural-restoration/) for the heavier model-based upscalers.

## Temporal upscalers

Temporal upscalers are a different breed. Instead of guessing detail from one image, they gather it across several frames: the game renders each frame with a tiny sub-pixel offset (jitter), and the upscaler uses the game's **depth** and **motion vectors** to line those frames up and rebuild a sharper picture. This is how modern games render at a fraction of the pixels and still look sharp.

MacLauncher includes:

- <span id="metalfx-temporal"></span>**[MetalFX Temporal](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metalfx)**, plus its [denoised variant](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metalfx/mtlfxtemporaldenoisedscaler) on macOS 26 or later;
- <span id="fsr-temporal"></span>**AMD [FSR 2.2](https://github.com/GPUOpen-Effects/FidelityFX-FSR2)**, **[FSR 3.1](https://github.com/GPUOpen-LibrariesAndSDKs/FidelityFX-SDK)** and **[FSR 4.1](https://github.com/UzenUPoZiTiV4ik/fsr4-native-macos)**, the last with Exact Fast, Lite and Lite 2 profiles on Apple Silicon with macOS 15 or later;
- <span id="arm-asr-and-snapdragon-gsr-2"></span>**[Arm ASR](https://github.com/arm/accuracy-super-resolution-generic-library)** and **[Snapdragon GSR 2](https://github.com/SnapdragonGameStudios/snapdragon-gsr)**;
- <span id="xess"></span>**[Intel XeSS](https://github.com/intel/xess)**;
- <span id="dlss-sr"></span>**DLSS SR** for scenes, through [MLX-DLSS](https://github.com/iamwavecut/MLX-DLSS).

The catch is that they need those depth, motion and camera inputs from the game itself. A finished picture alone isn't enough, so temporal upscalers are available only for games whose runtime hands MacLauncher that data. For everything else, the spatial and neural upscalers above are the way to go.

## Render size versus window size

Upscaling pairs naturally with rendering less. Two separate controls are involved:

- <span id="game-resolution"></span>**Game resolution** asks the game itself to render at a smaller size, which saves the game real work. It's available where the runtime supports it.
- <span id="downsample"></span>**Downsample** shrinks an image that has already been rendered. The game doesn't get faster, but every effect after it has fewer pixels to process, which matters a lot for neural models.

Either way, the window stays the same size; only the processing resolution changes. [Building a chain](/maclauncher/graphics/composing-effects/) has more on sizes, and the [graphics guide](https://github.com/procrastinine/maclauncher/blob/master/docs/GRAPHICS.md#rendering-processing-and-output-size) and [provider reference](https://github.com/procrastinine/maclauncher/blob/master/docs/graphics/PROVIDERS.md#spatial-resizing) have every option and requirement.
